Gender Differences in Financial Autonomy and Economic Dependency: A Comparative Empirical Analysis

A Quantitative Empirical Investigation Utilizing IBM SPSS Statistical Framework
ABSTRACT

This study investigates gender disparities in financial autonomy, economic dependency, and financial stress through a quantitative comparative research design (N = 150). Utilizing structured survey data and IBM SPSS analytical procedures, the research operationalizes key socio-economic indicators, including monthly gross income, individual expenditure, financial literacy, and multi-item Likert constructs measuring financial autonomy (α = .86) and financial stress (α = .91). Univariate analysis reveals that 56.1% of female respondents are classified as economically dependent compared to 29.4% of males. A Chi-Square test of independence confirms a statistically significant association between gender and economic dependency (χ²(1) = 10.78, p = .001, Cramér's V = .268). Independent samples t-tests demonstrate that males exhibit significantly higher financial autonomy scores (M = 3.75, SD = 0.78) than females (M = 3.29, SD = 0.85; t(148) = 3.39, p < .001). Multivariate ANOVA (MANOVA) further substantiates significant combined gender differences across outcome metrics (Wilks' Λ = .872, F(3, 146) = 7.14, p < .001). Multiple linear regression modeling indicates that monthly income (β = .522, p < .001), financial literacy (β = .314, p < .001), and female gender (β = -.108, p = .038) significantly account for 68.8% of the variance in personal financial autonomy (R² = .688). Binary logistic regression confirms that female gender increases the odds of economic dependency nearly fourfold (OR = 3.95, p = .006). The findings highlight systemic gender gaps in individual economic self-sufficiency and inform policy interventions targeting structural wage equity and financial education.

Keywords: Economic Dependency, Gender Disparities, Financial Autonomy, IBM SPSS Analysis, Multivariate Regression, Financial Literacy.

I. Introduction

1.1 Background of the Study

Individual economic independence is a fundamental determinant of socio-economic empowerment, agency, and household decision-making power. Despite structural advancements in education and labor force participation globally, economic dependency remains asymmetric across gender lines. Economic dependency refers to an individual's reliance on external institutional, familial, or spousal financial support to sustain living expenses. Conversely, financial autonomy represents the perceived and actual capacity to self-fund individual obligations and make independent financial decisions. Understanding the underlying mechanisms driving gender gaps in financial autonomy requires rigorous empirical examination of income levels, debt obligations, financial literacy, and institutional constraints.

1.2 Statement of the Problem

While national labor statistics frequently highlight macro-level wage gaps and labor force participation rates, micro-level disparities in individual economic dependency within households remain insufficiently quantified. Many individuals who earn nominal wages still experience high economic dependency due to disproportionate expense-to-income ratios, intra-household resource allocation imbalances, and lower financial literacy self-efficacy. Existing comparative research often relies solely on univariate cross-tabulations without integrating multivariate statistical frameworks that isolate the unique predictive effects of gender alongside income, age, education, and financial literacy. This empirical gap limits the development of targeted economic and social protection policies.

1.3 Research Questions

  1. Is there a statistically significant association between gender and economic dependency status?
  2. How do males and females differ across key continuous metrics, including monthly income, financial autonomy, and financial stress?
  3. To what extent do monthly income, financial literacy, age, and gender predict an individual's overall financial autonomy score?
  4. What is the odds ratio of being economically dependent for females relative to males when controlling for financial literacy, education, and age?

1.4 Significance of the Study

  • Theoretical Contribution: Extends the Relative Resources Theory by integrating individual financial literacy and scale-based financial stress metrics into predictive models of dependency.
  • Policy Contribution: Provides empirical evidence for policy interventions aimed at closing gender wage gaps, enhancing formal financial inclusion, and designing tailored financial literacy programs.
  • Methodological Contribution: Demonstrates a comprehensive IBM SPSS quantitative framework spanning univariate descriptive profiles, bivariate parametric/non-parametric comparative tests, and advanced multivariate regressions (N = 150).

1.5 Scope and Delimitations

  • Demographic Scope: Working-age adult respondents (21 to 58 years old, M = 31.95, SD = 6.94).
  • Methodological Delimitations: Cross-sectional quantitative survey design. The study focuses exclusively on formal quantifiable indicators (income, expenditure, Likert constructs) and does not cover qualitative intra-household negotiation narratives.

II. Literature Review & Conceptual Framework

2.1 Theoretical Foundation

  • Relative Resources Theory (Blood & Wolfe, 1960): Asserts that power and autonomy within a household are directly proportional to the tangible comparative resources (income, education, occupational status) contributed by each partner. Individuals contributing fewer financial resources exhibit greater economic dependency.
  • Human Capital Theory (Becker, 1964): Posits that wage differentials and subsequent financial reliance stem from variations in education, specialized skills, labor market experience, and financial knowledge.
  • Gender Role Socialization Theory: Explains how persistent institutional and cultural norms influence financial decision-making, risk tolerance, and economic self-sufficiency between men and women.

2.2 Empirical Literature Review

Prior studies consistently identify systemic gender disparities in earnings and resource control. Comparative research indicates that women frequently report higher levels of financial anxiety and lower financial self-efficacy, even when controlling for baseline educational attainment. Empirical work utilizing parametric t-tests and regression modeling demonstrates that financial literacy acts as a significant mediating variable; individuals with higher financial knowledge optimize expenditure-to-income ratios, lower reliance on informal debt, and maintain higher financial autonomy. However, multi-variable analyses that simultaneously examine structural factors (income, education) alongside cognitive factors (financial literacy) across gender remain sparse in current development literature.

2.3 Conceptual Framework & Hypotheses

Research Hypotheses

  • H01: Gender is independent of economic dependency status.
  • Ha1: Gender is significantly associated with economic dependency status.
  • H02: There is no significant difference in mean financial autonomy scores between males and females.
  • Ha2: Males exhibit significantly higher mean financial autonomy scores than females.
  • H03: Monthly income, financial literacy, age, and gender do not significantly predict financial autonomy.
  • Ha3: Monthly income, financial literacy, age, and gender significantly predict financial autonomy.
  • H04: Female gender does not significantly increase the likelihood of economic dependency in a logistic regression model.
  • Ha4: Female gender significantly increases the likelihood of economic dependency.

III. Research Methodology

3.1 Research Design

This study employs a cross-sectional quantitative comparative research design. Quantitative data were collected via structured survey instruments to facilitate statistical testing in IBM SPSS across univariate, bivariate, and multivariate domains.

3.2 Population and Sampling

The sampling frame comprises 150 stratified adult respondents (n = 68 males, n = 82 females). Sampling was conducted using stratified random sampling to ensure representation across educational attainment levels (High School, Bachelor's, Master's/PhD) and employment categories.

3.3 Data Collection Instrument

The primary instrument was a structured closed-ended questionnaire divided into three modules: Demographics, Financial Metrics, and Psychometric Scales (5-Point Likert: 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree).

V. Discussion

The quantitative results establish structural gender disparities across both objective financial metrics and subjective psychometric constructs. Female respondents exhibit a 56.1% economic dependency rate compared to 29.4% among males. Crucially, after controlling for income, age, literacy, and education, the female gender dummy remains a significant driver in logistic dependency modeling (OR = 3.95, p = .006).

VI. Conclusion & Recommendations

  • Targeted Financial Education Programs: Deploy focused financial literacy workshops targeting budget optimization, debt management, and investment strategies for women.
  • Institutional Equal Pay Audits: Employers should conduct regular wage equity audits to eliminate gender wage compression.
  • Formal Financial Inclusion: Financial institutions should establish tailored credit facilities to assist women in building independent asset portfolios.
